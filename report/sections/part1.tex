\section{Introduction}
\writtenby{Lithma Perera}

Extreme wildfires cause loss of life, property and ecosystems on every vegetated continent,
and a warming climate is making the conditions for them more common \cite{bowman2017}. During a
response, incident teams need up-to-date, close-range views of the active fire front and of
assets at risk such as roads, settlements and power lines. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
are increasingly used for this task because they are cheap, fast to deploy and safe to fly
over hazardous terrain \cite{yuan2015}. A single drone, however, has limited endurance. A
small fleet can cover more ground, but only if its members coordinate: they must spread out
over the areas that most need attention, return before their batteries run out, and take
turns at a shared charging point.

This project studies that coordination problem in simulation. Three battery-limited drones
are commanded by one central controller. Fires spread stochastically under changing wind, and
every location has an inspection age that grows until a drone flies over it. The goal is to
keep high-priority locations, especially burning cells and the fire front, recently
inspected for the whole mission while never losing a drone.

The problem is hard for three reasons. First, decisions are joint: five commands per drone
give $5^3 = 125$ joint actions at every step, and the value of one drone's move depends on
where the other two are. Second, consequences are delayed: flying too far from base costs
nothing immediately but strands the drone many steps later. Third, one charger creates
contention, so the fleet must stagger its returns. These properties make the task a
sequential decision problem under uncertainty with a clear numerical objective, which is the
setting reinforcement learning (RL) is designed for \cite{sutton2018}. Prior work has applied
deep RL to wildfire surveillance with autonomous aircraft \cite{julian2019} and to fire-front
monitoring with multiple UAVs \cite{viseras2021}. Unlike those studies, we give every
controller the full state, so that RL can be compared fairly with conventional planning that
uses the same information.

Our contributions are: (i) a Gymnasium environment for fleet inspection with stochastic fire,
wind, batteries, collisions and a shared charger; (ii) implementations of DQN, REINFORCE and
A2C trained under one protocol with a feasibility mask; (iii) a frozen 40$\times$40 comparison
on 20 unseen worlds against Greedy, rolling-horizon, sweep and random controllers, with
tuning and sensitivity studies; and (iv) a measured explanation of why the learned policies
did not improve on random feasible behaviour. The code, the executed notebook and all archived
results accompany this report \cite{project2026}.

\section{Markov Decision Process and Environment}
\writtenby{Lithma Perera}

We formulate the task as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP)
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, H)$ with horizon $H = 300$ steps and
discount $\gamma = 0.99$ \cite{sutton2018}. The environment follows the Gymnasium
\texttt{Env} interface. All values below are those of the 40$\times$40 main study; the earlier
20$\times$20 development study used a smaller, shorter version (horizon 160, battery 80).

\subsection{State and observation}

The state is fully observed. The observation is a dictionary of three arrays, all scaled to
$[0,1]$:
\begin{itemize}
\item five $40\times40$ maps: burning cells, remaining fuel, terrain flammability, static
priority and inspection age;
\item a $3\times4$ array with each drone's row, column, battery and a lost flag;
\item 12 global values: wind direction (one-hot over calm and four directions), charger owner
(one-hot over none and three drones), base position and the fraction of the mission left.
\end{itemize}
Keeping the fire, fuel and age information as spatial maps preserves the neighbourhood
structure that convolutional networks exploit in pixel-based RL \cite{mnih2015}. Every
quantity that influences the next-state distribution is included, including inspection ages
and remaining time, so the Markov property holds and no observation history is needed. This
is a deliberate simplification of real operations, where fire is only partly visible; we
return to it in Section~\ref{sec:future}. Before the network, the drone and base coordinates
are also drawn as four binary maps, giving nine spatial channels and 24 vector inputs.

\subsection{Action space}

The action $a_t \in \{0, \dots, 124\}$ encodes one of five commands (stay, north, south,
west, east) for each drone. A single joint action lets a central controller coordinate the
fleet directly and lets us use standard discrete-action algorithms without changing them.
Its cost is a size that grows as $5^N$ with fleet size $N$; Section~\ref{sec:improvements}
discusses factorised alternatives. Inspection is automatic: every cell within a Chebyshev
radius of two cells of an active drone is inspected after it moves.

\subsection{Transition model}

Each step applies the following updates in order, with all randomness drawn from the world's
seeded generator.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Movement.} Moves off the grid are ignored. If two drones would enter the same
non-base cell or swap cells, both moves are rejected and counted as collision attempts.
\item \emph{Energy and charging.} A move costs 1 unit and hovering 0.25 from a capacity of
$B = 160$. One drone staying at the base charges by 8 units per step; when several wait, the
one with the lowest battery is served first. A drone whose battery reaches zero away from
base is lost permanently.
\item \emph{Inspection.} All ages increase by one; cells under a sensor footprint are reset
to zero.
\item \emph{Fire and wind.} A burning cell burns one unit of fuel per step and goes out when
its fuel is exhausted. An unburnt cell $i$ with fuel ignites with probability
\begin{equation}
P_i = 1 - \prod_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i \cap \mathcal{F}_t}
\bigl(1 - \min(1,\, q\, \omega_{ji}\, \tau_i)\bigr),
\label{eq:spread}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_i$ are the four neighbours of $i$, $\mathcal{F}_t$ the burning cells,
$q = 0.04$ the spread probability, $\tau_i \in [0.4, 1]$ the terrain flammability, and
$\omega_{ji} = 2$ when the spread from $j$ to $i$ is downwind and 1 otherwise. The wind
changes to a random direction with probability 0.06 per step.
\end{enumerate}
An episode ends at the horizon or when all three drones are lost. Each world fixes the
terrain, fuel, three high-priority zones and three initial fires. The physical constants are
illustrative rather than calibrated.

\subsection{Reward design}

Inspection quality is defined with priority weights $w_i = p_i(1 + 2b_i + f_i)$, where
$p_i \in \{1, 3\}$ marks priority zones, $b_i$ burning cells and $f_i$ cells on the fire
front. With inspection age $a_i$ and deadline $D = 30$, the \emph{weighted freshness} and
\emph{overdue fraction} are
\begin{equation}
F_t = \frac{\sum_i w_i \max(0, 1 - a_i/D)}{\sum_i w_i}, \quad
O_t = \frac{\sum_i w_i \, \mathbb{1}[a_i > D]}{\sum_i w_i}.
\end{equation}
The reward is
\begin{equation}
r_t = G_t - 0.1\,O_t - 0.02\,\frac{E_t}{B} - 0.2\,K_t - 5\,L_t - 0.1\,(H - t - 1)\,
\mathbb{1}[\text{fleet lost}],
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $G_t = \sum_{i \in C_t} w_i \min(1, a_i/D) / \sum_i w_i$ is the weighted staleness
removed by this step's inspections $C_t$, $E_t$ the energy used, $K_t$ the collision
attempts and $L_t$ the newly lost drones.

Each term targets part of the goal. The inspection gain $G_t$ is dense: it rewards a useful
inspection immediately rather than only at the end of a 300-step mission, which shortens the
credit-assignment path and should speed up learning. Because it is larger for stale and
high-weight cells, it favours revisiting the fire front rather than circling one area. The
overdue term keeps pressure on every location, the energy term discourages pointless
movement, and the collision term penalises blocked commands. The loss penalty and the
mission-failure term charge the remaining horizon as fully overdue, so losing the fleet early
is never better than flying the whole mission. All terms are part of the task objective
rather than added shaping, so they define the optimal policy instead of altering it; shaping
that is not potential-based can change which policy is optimal \cite{ng1999}. For evaluation
we report $F_t$ and $O_t$ averaged over the full horizon, return, energy, drones lost and
decision time.

\section{Reinforcement Learning Agents}
\writtenby{Sulakna Weerasinghe}

\subsection{Choice of agents}

We chose one agent from each of the three main families of model-free RL, so that a failure
could not be blamed on one family's weaknesses. DQN is value-based and off-policy: its replay
buffer reuses each transition many times, which suits an expensive simulator with a discrete
action set \cite{mnih2015}. REINFORCE is a Monte Carlo policy-gradient method: it gives
unbiased gradients of the expected return and learns a stochastic policy directly
\cite{williams1992}. A2C is an actor--critic method: its critic bootstraps value estimates to
reduce the variance of the policy gradient and allows updates within an episode
\cite{mnih2016}. Comparing them shows whether value estimation, full returns or bootstrapping
copes best with long missions and a large joint action space.

All three share one network: two strided convolutions (16 and 32 filters) over the nine
spatial channels, concatenated with the 24 vector inputs, a 128-unit hidden layer and a
125-way output, which gives Q-values for DQN and logits for the policies. REINFORCE and A2C
add a scalar value head (435{,}006 parameters in total at 40$\times$40). All agents use Adam,
$\gamma = 0.99$ and gradient-norm clipping at 0.5.

\subsection{Update rules}

\emph{DQN} minimises the Huber loss between $Q_\theta(s,a)$ and the target
$y = r + \gamma \max_{a' \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{f}}(s')} Q_{\bar\theta}(s', a')$, where
$Q_{\bar\theta}$ is a target network copied every 100 steps and $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{f}}$ the
feasible actions. It samples minibatches of 32 from a 2{,}000-transition replay buffer and
explores with $\epsilon$ decayed from 1 to 0.05 over 40{,}000 steps.

\emph{REINFORCE} with a learned baseline updates once per episode with
$\nabla_\theta \sum_t \gamma^t \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\,(G_t - V_\phi(s_t))$, where $G_t$ is
the discounted reward-to-go and the baseline $V_\phi$ is fitted by squared error.

\emph{A2C} updates every 32 steps using $n$-step returns bootstrapped from the critic,
the advantage $A_t = G^{(n)}_t - V_\phi(s_t)$, a value loss weighted 0.5 and an entropy bonus
of 0.05. It is the synchronous, single-environment form of the method in \cite{mnih2016}.
Full update equations are given in the Appendix.

\subsection{Feasibility mask}

The first versions of all three agents chose freely among the 125 actions and lost most of the
fleet (Section~\ref{sec:development}), while every conventional controller kept its drones
because it only proposed safe moves. We therefore applied the same feasibility rule to the
learners. A move is feasible only if the drone keeps enough battery to fly back to base
afterwards; a drone low on battery must move towards base; a drone at base below full charge
must stay and charge; and joint actions that would collide are excluded. In the rare case
where no joint action remains, an emergency rule moves the drones with the smallest margin
home first. The mask is applied when acting and inside the DQN target, and infeasible logits
are set to the lowest finite value so they receive zero probability. This follows shielding
in safe RL \cite{alshiekh2018} and invalid-action masking in policy-gradient methods, which
keeps the policy gradient valid \cite{huang2022}. At reset, 73 of the 125 joint actions are
feasible.

\section{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\writtenby{Sayuru Bopitiya}

Tuning had two stages. At 20$\times$20, a one-seed screen trained each agent for 200 episodes
per setting and scored the final policy on five validation worlds. It chose REINFORCE's
learning rate ($10^{-3}$, freshness 0.131 against 0.114 at $10^{-4}$) and entropy weight (0,
0.132 against 0.114 at 0.01), and A2C's entropy weight (0.05, 0.164 against 0.126 at 0.01) and
rollout length (32 steps). DQN scored best at $10^{-3}$ in this screen, but we kept
$10^{-4}$ for the main study because the larger rate had produced the Q-value blow-up above.
These settings were frozen before the 40$\times$40 study.

Because the 40$\times$40 rates were not tuned on the larger grid, we later trained each agent
from scratch at one third, one and three times its main-study rate (one seed, 300 episodes),
scoring each on 20 separate validation worlds (Table~\ref{tab:lr}). None of the rates closed
the gap to Greedy. DQN at its main-study rate was the best fit and slightly ahead of masked
random (0.098 against 0.091 freshness, $-24.00$ against $-25.14$ return), but this margin is
smaller than the standard deviation across worlds (about 0.015). REINFORCE improved with a
larger rate and A2C was flat over a ten-fold range. With one seed per setting, differences of
about 0.01 are within seed-to-seed variation \cite{henderson2018}, so the sweep cannot rule
out the learning rate as one contributing factor. It does show that a three-fold change in
either direction was not enough on its own.

\section{Main Study at 40$\times$40}
\writtenby{Sayuru Bopitiya}

\subsection{Protocol}

The implementation, settings, budget and success criteria were fixed before any main-study
fit started. Each agent was trained with seeds 0, 1 and 2 for 300 episodes on training worlds
0--299 (90{,}000 environment steps per fit). The final weights of all nine fits were then
evaluated once, acting greedily over feasible actions, on 20 unseen worlds (22000--22019).
Greedy, the lawnmower sweep, random and masked random flew the same worlds, giving 260
missions; the rolling-horizon planner was added later on the same worlds. Greedy scores every
feasible joint action by the priority-weighted staleness its sensors would remove and picks
the best. The planner simulates eight candidate actions four steps ahead over two sampled
fire futures. For a learner, results are averaged over worlds within each seed and the
standard deviation is taken across the three seed means; for a baseline it is across worlds.
An independent replay reproduced all 75{,}911 evaluation steps exactly, including every
action, mask, state hash and reward.

\subsection{Training behaviour}

Fig.~\ref{fig:learning} shows the training returns. DQN improved steadily during the first
100 episodes, from a mean of $-25.1$ over the first 50 episodes to $-23.8$ over the last 50, and
then levelled off. REINFORCE ($-25.1$ to $-25.0$) and A2C ($-25.3$ to $-25.4$) showed no
trend: their returns moved within the noise of individual missions, and the three seeds of
each agent did not agree on a direction. Training freshness followed the same pattern, rising
from 0.089 to 0.101 for DQN but not for the two policy-gradient agents.

\section{Critical Review}
\label{sec:review}
\writtenby{Sayuru Bopitiya}

\subsection{Was the problem solved?}

Only partly. The engineering goals were met: the environment, three agents, a fixed protocol,
unseen-world evaluation and an exact audit were all completed, and every learned policy flew
full missions without losing a drone. The learning goal was not met. In every comparison the
learned policies inspected no better than random feasible actions, and a one-step heuristic
was more than twice as good. The zero-loss result must therefore be credited to the mask, not
to learning: masked random, which has no learning at all, also lost no drones. Without the
masked-random control, the safety result could easily have been misread as learned
competence.

\subsection{Why the learners stayed at masked-random level}

All three agents learn from differences in reward between actions. To measure how large these
are, we froze the state every tenth step under random feasible play, tried every feasible
joint action, and recorded the immediate reward. At 40$\times$40 the best action beats the
second-best by only 0.0005 on average, and the standard deviation across actions is 0.002.
The standard deviation of the mission return across worlds is 0.87, about 430 times larger
(130 times at 20$\times$20). The useful signal is therefore buried in noise: REINFORCE updates
once per episode from a single noisy return; A2C bootstraps from a critic that must itself
resolve these tiny differences; and DQN must estimate Q-values whose gaps between actions are
smaller than its approximation error. This matches the flat learning curves of the two
policy-gradient agents, and the fact that DQN's training gains did not transfer to unseen
worlds.

The joint action space makes this worse. One drone's good move is rewarded together with the
other two drones' choices, so the agent cannot easily tell which drone deserved the credit.
Greedy, by contrast, computes exactly the quantity the reward measures, so it does not need to
learn it from noisy samples. Three seeds and 90{,}000 steps per fit are also a small budget
for deep RL, where results vary strongly between seeds \cite{henderson2018}. However, the
learning-rate sweep and the DQN plateau suggest that more compute alone would not close a
gap of this size.

\subsection{Implications and validity}

The study shows that a well-designed heuristic with full information is a strong baseline
for fleet inspection, and that safe behaviour obtained through masking should not be
reported as learning success. The conclusions hold for this simulator only: fire, wind and
battery values are illustrative, the state is fully observed, and the 20$\times$20 and
40$\times$40 studies differ in several settings at once, so their difference is not a
controlled scaling result. Part~II proposes changes that target the measured causes.
