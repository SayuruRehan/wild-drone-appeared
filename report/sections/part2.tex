\section{Limitations and Potential Improvements}
\label{sec:improvements}
\writtenby{all members; each subsection names its author}

Part~I identified three limitations of the current work, each supported by our own results:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[L1] \emph{Credit assignment over a joint action.} One network scores all 125 joint
actions, so a single scalar reward must teach it which of three drones acted well. The
learned policies matched masked random on unseen worlds (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
\item[L2] \emph{A weak, noisy learning signal.} The immediate reward separates the best
and second-best actions by about 0.0005, while mission returns vary 430 times more than
rewards across actions; the policy-gradient learning curves were flat
(Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}).
\item[L3] \emph{Learning from scratch with a small budget.} Every agent started from random
weights with 90{,}000 steps, although a strong controller (Greedy, 0.221 freshness) was
available; DQN's training gains levelled off after about 100 episodes.
\end{enumerate}
We propose one structural improvement for each. None of them is a change of hyperparameters.

\subsection{Improvement 1: Factorised credit assignment (L1)}
\writtenby{Lithma Perera}

\emph{Proposal.} Replace the 125-way joint head with per-drone utility networks that share
the spatial encoder, and combine them into a joint value with a learned mixing network, as in
QMIX \cite{rashid2018}. Each drone's utility depends on its own position and battery plus the
shared fire and age maps, and the mixer is conditioned on the full state. Because the mixer is
monotonic in each drone's utility, the joint greedy action can be found by maximising each
drone's utility separately, so the output grows as $5N$ rather than $5^N$. The feasibility
mask would be applied per drone, with the collision check kept as a joint filter. A
policy-gradient alternative is a centralised critic with a counterfactual baseline (COMA),
which compares the joint value with the value obtained when only one drone's action is
replaced by an average over its alternatives \cite{foerster2018coma}.

\emph{Justification.} Both methods were designed for cooperative teams that share one reward,
which is exactly our situation. QMIX outperformed independent learners and simpler value
decompositions on cooperative StarCraft micromanagement tasks \cite{rashid2018}, and COMA's
counterfactual advantage reduced the variance caused by teammates' actions in the same domain
\cite{foerster2018coma}. In our task, a drone that inspects a stale fire front while another
wanders currently receives the same scalar signal as both; a per-drone utility can attribute
the gain to the drone whose footprint caused it.

\emph{Evaluation.} Keep the environment, mask, budget, seeds and world split of the main study
and change only the action and value structure. Report freshness, the number of
cells inspected by more than one drone at once, charger waiting time and the gap to Greedy.
If freshness stays at masked-random level, credit assignment is not the main bottleneck,
which would itself be a useful result.

\subsection{Improvement 2: Potential-based shaping for inspection urgency (L2)}
\writtenby{Sulakna Weerasinghe}

\emph{Proposal.} Add a training-only shaping term
$F(s, a, s') = \gamma\,\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ with a potential that measures how well placed the
fleet is to serve the most urgent locations:
\begin{equation}
\Phi(s) = -\lambda \sum_{k=1}^{3} \min_{i \in U(s)} d(x_k, i)
- \mu \sum_{k=1}^{3} \max\bigl(0,\, d(x_k, \text{base}) - \beta_k\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $U(s)$ are the most urgent cells (high weight and close to their deadline), $d$ is grid
distance, $x_k$ and $\beta_k$ are drone $k$'s position and the distance its battery allows, and
$\lambda, \mu$ are small scales. The first term rewards moving towards urgent cells before they
are reached; the second rewards keeping a safe return margin. Evaluation always uses the
unshaped reward in (\ref{eq:reward}).

\emph{Justification.} Ng et al.\ proved that adding $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ leaves the set
of optimal policies unchanged, whereas arbitrary shaping can create loops that earn reward
without completing the task \cite{ng1999}. The term is useful precisely where our reward is
weakest: today a drone receives nothing until its sensor reaches a stale cell, so the ten
steps it takes to get there are credited only through noisy returns. The potential difference
rewards each of those steps directly, which increases the gap between good and poor actions
without changing what is optimal. For our finite horizon, the potential must be set to zero
at termination so that ending early cannot earn extra credit.

\emph{Evaluation.} Repeat the reward-signal measurement of Section~\ref{sec:review} with the
shaped reward to check that the gap between the best and second-best actions grows. Then train
with and without shaping under the main-study protocol and compare learning speed and
unshaped test freshness. A higher shaped return without higher test freshness would mean the
potential is poorly chosen.

\subsection{Improvement 3: Learning from demonstrations (L3)}
\writtenby{Sayuru Bopitiya}

\emph{Proposal.} Start DQN from Greedy's behaviour instead of from random weights, using Deep
Q-learning from Demonstrations (DQfD) \cite{hester2018}. Greedy and planner missions on
training worlds fill a demonstration buffer that is never overwritten. The network is first
pre-trained on it with the usual TD loss plus a large-margin loss that makes the demonstrated
action score higher than the others, then continues learning from its own experience while
still sampling demonstrations. Prioritised replay \cite{schaul2016} would sample rare but
important transitions, such as charger hand-overs and late returns, more often than routine
patrol steps.

\emph{Justification.} DQfD started from much better performance than DQN on almost all Atari
games it was tested on and exceeded its demonstrator on several, because the TD loss allows the agent to improve on demonstrations
rather than only copy them \cite{hester2018}. Prioritised replay improved DQN on most Atari
games by replaying transitions with large TD errors \cite{schaul2016}. Our own 20$\times$20
imitation experiments showed that copying Greedy alone generalised poorly (9--18\% agreement on
new worlds), which is the weakness DQfD addresses by combining imitation with RL on the
agent's own states.

\emph{Evaluation.} Compare DQfD with plain DQN under the same step budget, counting
demonstration steps against it. Plot freshness against steps to measure sample efficiency, and
test whether the final policy exceeds Greedy on unseen worlds, matches it, or falls back
towards masked random once demonstrations are diluted.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
\writtenby{all members; each subsection names its author}

The following directions do not repair the limitations above. They extend the problem towards
real fire operations with methods we have not used.

\subsection{Extension 1: Partial observability and belief maps}
\writtenby{Lithma Perera}

Our controller sees the true fire map at every step. In practice, fire is known only where a
drone or other sensor has recently looked, and smoke causes missed and false detections. We
would hide the fire state, model a camera with detection and false-alarm rates, and require
the fleet to keep a belief map of where fire is likely to be. The task then becomes a
partially observable MDP (POMDP), and the controller must trade inspecting known fire against
searching areas where the belief is uncertain. Exact POMDP solutions are intractable for state
spaces of this size, and Hauskrecht reviews value-function approximations that make them
practical \cite{hauskrecht2000}. Julian and Kochenderfer showed that deep RL can learn
distributed wildfire surveillance when each aircraft acts on a belief map of the fire built
from its own observations \cite{julian2019}. We would compare a belief-based learner with an information-gain planner
and the Greedy rule applied to the belief, and report detection delay, belief error and
freshness measured against the true fire.

\subsection{Extension 2: Decentralised fleets with limited communication}
\writtenby{Sulakna Weerasinghe}

We assume one controller with instant access to every drone. A larger fleet would more
likely have each drone deciding from its own sensors and exchanging short messages over an
unreliable link. We would give each drone a local view, add a communication channel with
bandwidth limits, delay and dropouts, and model the problem as a decentralised POMDP.
Foerster et al.\ showed that agents can learn useful communication protocols end to end, by
reinforcement or by back-propagating through the channel during centralised training
\cite{foerster2016}. Centralised training with decentralised execution, as in QMIX
\cite{rashid2018}, keeps the advantage of a shared critic during training while removing the
need for a central controller in flight. The study would measure freshness and safety as
message rates fall and links fail, against a baseline in which drones share nothing.

\subsection{Extension 3: Calibrated and heterogeneous operations}
\writtenby{Sayuru Bopitiya}

Our fire model, terrain and battery values are illustrative. To move towards operational
use, we would drive the simulator with real terrain and fuel maps, calibrated spread under
recorded weather, drones with different endurance and sensors, and several charging sites.
Policies would be trained with domain randomisation over these parameters, which Tobin et
al.\ used to transfer models trained only on simulated images to a real robot
\cite{tobin2017}, and tested on held-out regions and weather regimes. Viseras et al.\
provide a precedent for monitoring a fire front with several UAVs using deep Q-learning
\cite{viseras2021}. Results would report freshness of the actual front, energy, robustness to
sensor and drone failures, and decision latency, which our planner results show can become
the limiting factor.
